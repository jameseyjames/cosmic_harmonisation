## From the abstract (second-hand)
- Proposes a globally harmonised but locally adaptable index: the International Brain Health Index (IBHI).
- Uses the Alkire-Foster counting method (from multidimensional poverty measurement).
- Steps: choose cognitive domains and indicators; define "deprivation" with local cut-offs; count deprivations to classify the severity and type of brain health loss, for individuals and populations.
- Aim: equity and local cultural relevance in LMICs, where standard neuropsychological tests may not be validated.
- Affiliations mentioned: Aga Khan University, Dartmouth.
- Not confirmed: the poverty-index link in your description may be this Alkire-Foster idea (a multidimensional poverty method) rather than adjusting for a poverty score. Check.

## Inferences: how it could help our project (to verify)
1. **Categorical fallback (Tier 2/3).** Where cohorts lack overlap for IRT/CFA linking, locally defined cut-offs per domain could give comparable categories (impaired or not, per domain) instead of a continuous score.
2. **Domain counts as a global measure.** Number of domains impaired is a global indicator that does not need the same tests everywhere. This matches our "minimum domains" question.
3. **Local norms.** Cut-offs defined within each cohort address education, language and culture, which matter for scaling beyond Western cohorts (a concern raised with Susanne).
4. **Transparent rules.** Fits our decision log and provenance principles.

## Limitations to weigh (my view)
- Categorising loses information and power compared with latent scores.
- Local cut-offs make comparison across cohorts a comparison of relative position, not equated ability.
- Cut-off choice drives results, so it needs justification and sensitivity analyses.

## Meeting notes (first-hand, 2026-10-30)

### Her approach
- Categorical "meta-metrics" for brain health: is impairment present, how severe, and what is the profile across domains. This mirrors the multidimensional poverty index (present, intensity, profile of deprivations) and was informed by epilepsy classification.
- Loses some data, but avoids many statistical assumptions. It can extend beyond cognition.
- So far tested only on 10/66 data. She plans to test robustness across other datasets, measures and cohorts.
- Statistical co-calibration works but has limits. It needs well-linked, similarly administered items.
- Motivation: in LMICs people are assessed very late, and cross-country comparison of individuals is risky or unethical. The approach supports population-level forecasting and tracking.
- Context: education effects are huge across cohorts and cultures, and tests like animal fluency and word recall are not enough in LMICs. Local measurement bias adds imprecision.

### Her advice for COSMIC
- Just try it, and check whether it gives something meaningful.
- Define impairment relative to normative levels, not the sample distribution, because we do not know who is normal.
- Build regression-based norms from normative reference groups. With a large enough sample, split into normative and test groups.
- Map measures to domains (see the WHO measure-mapping work by Kathryn/Catherine), derive reference groups, then apply the actuarial approach.
- Cross-walking is needed. Verbal fluency can stand in for executive function when data are lacking.
- Let the existing data define what is pragmatic, rather than starting with a Delphi process.
- HCAP differs because it harmonised prospectively, with the same batteries and controlled interviewer training.

### Consortium
- She is considering a consortium around this harmonisation goal, with Gheorghe. COSMIC could contribute pragmatic solutions.
- She is open to our suggestions for next steps and plans to apply it to more datasets.
